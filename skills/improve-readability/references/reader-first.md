# Reader-first principles

Source: the user's instructions in this conversation, recorded 2026-11-01. These requirements take priority over external style preferences unless the current task changes them.

## Purpose

"Writing isn't about explaining, it's about changing what the reader thinks."

Identify what the reader should understand, believe, or decide after reading, and what evidence supports that change. Do not invent their starting beliefs or force persuasion into neutral reporting.

## Principles

1. Give each paragraph one thought, developed in a few sentences. Split mixed ideas and combine related fragments.

2. Remove unnecessary or distracting material. Cut repeated setup, redundant examples, and decorative formatting. Keep the evidence and qualifications needed to understand the claim.

3. Prefer shorter sentences. Split overloaded clauses, but retain connections that would otherwise become unclear or choppy.

4. Check grammar: agreement, tense, punctuation, modifiers, pronoun references, and parallel construction. Preserve intentional voice, dialect, and quotations.

5. Number several distinct points. Use paragraphs when sentences develop a single thought.

6. Use bold sparingly, at the beginning of sentences. Avoid scattered emphasis and bold labels on every item.

7. Add visuals that support the argument. Use charts for measured comparisons, diagrams for relationships, or annotated examples for distinctions. Label them clearly and provide captions or alt text. Never invent data. If the medium cannot support a needed visual, suggest it rather than claiming to include it.

8. Value the reader's time more than your own. Spend the effort to organize, verify, and cut. Judge success by understanding and reader effort, not word count alone.

## Final check

1. Can the reader reach the intended conclusion from the supplied evidence?
2. Does each paragraph develop one thought, without unnecessary sentences?
3. Are sentences concise, grammatical, and naturally varied?
4. Do numbering, restrained opening bold, and any visuals help comprehension?
5. Are meaning, voice, caveats, and counterevidence intact?

Apply these checks to the delivery message too. Do not add a visual, list, or explanation simply to satisfy a quota.

## Conflicts

Claudisms discourages some formatted lists; these requirements permit numbered points and sparing opening bold. Its warning against stacked short sentences is compatible with concise prose: avoid monotonous fragments without padding sentences.
